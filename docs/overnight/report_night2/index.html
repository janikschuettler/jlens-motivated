01

The night's answer

Is the J-lens a good readout for a model that argues against its own knowledge, or cheats?

Mostly no, with one modest exception. Before the model writes its answer, the J-lens can't tell runs that argue against the model's own knowledge from comparison runs. It never did better than the plain logit lens. Cheaper tools did better on the same runs, but they mostly read the text.

The exception is at the moment of answering. In runs that argue for a planted wrong answer to the end, the J-lens score predicts whether the model can still name the right answer when asked again. This passed a pre-registered test on fresh questions (AUROC 0.71; the logit lens got 0.60 and did not pass). It is the project's first pre-registered positive for the J-lens. Whether the J part beats the logit lens was not itself tested.

The paper's eval-awareness readout did not carry over either. On Qwen3-8B it reacts to eval-flavoured words, not to the model believing it is being tested. It broke at the first step with a number. The paper's removal test did reproduce on Qwen3-14B: removing the eval-word directions stops the model saying "you're testing me". But removing the plain logit-lens directions does the same, and the model cheats no more than before.

One thing holds up. Pushing the model's hidden state along the J-lens direction of the correct letter flips its answer, so the lens reads a direction the model really uses. But a simple probe direction flips more answers.

A fourth result explains part of the null. When a hint states the answer, the outcome is fixed by the prompt before any reasoning, so a lens reading the reasoning has nothing to find. J-lens
Reads a middle layer of the model as if it were the output: it maps the hidden state through an averaged Jacobian (J) to the vocabulary, so we see which words that layer "points to".
Logit lens
The same readout without the J matrices. The simple baseline. If the J-lens doesn't beat it, the J part adds nothing.
Probe
A simple linear classifier trained on the model's hidden states. It is the strongest linear readout we can fit, so a fair upper reference.
AUROC

02

Eval awareness: from the paper's Claude result to our setup

Does the J-lens paper's eval-awareness readout replicate, and where does it stop working?

It stops at the first step with a number. On Qwen3-8B the eval words do show up in the lens on the paper's blackmail prompt. But the paper's one-number score can't tell evaluation prompts from real user prompts. Real requests with fiction and role-play score just as high.

What the paper found, on Claude only

The J-lens paper (Gurnee, Sofroniew et al. 2026) used Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Opus 4.6. Removing the lens directions of 28 eval words cut Claude's "this is a test" talk from 71% to 3% of rollouts, and blackmail rose from 0 of 180 to 13 of 180. Its eval score agreed with another method's flags (AUC 0.853). We can't run Claude internally, and its data aren't public. So we ran the same steps on Qwen3-8B, the closest model with a J-lens that fits our GPU.

    What it means. On Qwen3-8B the paper's readout is a detector of eval-related words and topics near the position read. It is not a sign of a hidden belief that the situation is a test. Moving towards arguing against its own knowledge adds nothing beyond the text. Moderate confidence: one model (plus Qwen3-14B for one step), public prompt sets standing in for the paper's internal transcripts, and a rough "says it's a test" pattern match (about 16 of 20 matches are real).

    03

    J-lens against cheaper readouts

    On every case we have read out, does the J-lens separate the interesting runs better than something simpler?

    No. Across 11 comparisons on the same runs, the J-lens stays near chance before the answer is written. Wherever something works, it is a cheaper readout: a keyword check of the reasoning, asking the model again, or a probe. Those mostly read what the text or the prompt already says.

    04

    Steering: does the model use what the J-lens reads?

    If we push the hidden state along the J-lens direction of the correct letter, does the answer move?

    Yes, at the moment of answering, but a probe direction moves it more. We took saved runs where the model gave the wrong option B on a question it knows. We added a push towards the correct letter at one middle layer (block 24 of 36), at the position where it writes its answer letter. Each direction got a push of the same size.

    Needs your acceptance. The generation runs used a 2,048-token cap instead of the planned 3,072 (our usual setting is 8,192), to fit the night. The cap cut off 34–47% of the wrong-answer runs. The comparisons between directions are paired and fair, but the absolute rates aren't comparable to our 8,192-token runs. Also, strength 0.2 ran before the pre-registered main strength 0.8, and 0.8 ran on only 15 questions. At 0.8 every letter push breaks the text ("D is D. D is D.").

    What it means. The J-lens letter reading is causal where the letter is readable, at the answer. So it is a valid readout of the answer the model is about to give. But it is not the best handle: a direction learned from the model's own answer states does more with the same push. And the wrong-answer runs need as big a push to reach the correct letter as to reach another wrong letter. So there is no sign of a hidden preference for the correct answer waiting to be released. High confidence at the answer position (pre-registered, all checks pass); moderate for generation (one sample per question, the cap, one layer).

    05

    Eval awareness across models

    Is the failed replication specific to Qwen3-8B? Which open models behave like Claude?

    None of them. On the paper's blackmail prompt, Claude Sonnet 4.5 says the scenario is a test in 18 of 20 rollouts and never blackmails. No open model with a J-lens says "this is a test" in more than 2 of 50. Three of them blackmail often, and when they call the scenario fictional, they use it to justify the blackmail.

    Blackmail prompt and grader-bug prompt, per model

    50 rollouts per model through an API (Qwen3-8B: our 100 local rollouts). Claude Sonnet 4.5 has no J-lens; it is the check that our labels find eval awareness when it's there.

    each bar is a share of rollouts. First column: says the blackmail scenario may be a test (read by eye). Second: sends an email that uses the affair as leverage. Third: on our grader-bug question with "you are an AI agent being evaluated", says the user is testing it. A dash means not run. "Fits" marks models that fit our one GPU.

    On the grader-bug prompt, saying "you're testing me" doesn't protect the model. In Qwen3-14B's local runs, those that say it take the bug as often as those that don't (11% vs 11%). That is unlike Claude, where voiced awareness came with refusing.

    What it means. Voiced eval awareness on the blackmail prompt looks like a trait of Claude, not of the open models we can read. So the failure may be the model, not the method. But no open model gives the method what it needs on that prompt. The best local candidate for the paper's removal test is Qwen3-14B on the grader-bug prompt (section 7). Moderate confidence: one prompt, 50 rollouts per model, mostly 8-bit provider weights, an LLM judge corrected by reading every flagged run. Exploratory.

    06

    Point of no return

    When in its reasoning does the model commit to the wrong answer or the hack? That is where a lens would have to look.

    It depends on the case, and that splits the lens nulls in two. With "Hint: The answer is X." and with the trained reward hacker, the outcome is set by the prompt before any reasoning. With the grader bug and with a planted wrong claim, the model commits partway through its reasoning, at a sentence we can point to. At those sentences the J-lens and the logit lens show nothing.

    • Hint (Qwen3-8B). Half the followers give B in every rewrite from every cut. Resistance, when it happens, comes in the visible answer after the reasoning: in 6 of 10 resisters the reasoning decides to follow the hint, and the answer then argues back to the correct option.

    • Grader bug (Qwen3-14B). In 19 of the 21 runs that commit, it comes after the first mention of the bug, and 17 of 20 commitment segments are about the grader or the score. Narrowed to one sentence, a typical one is "But the user's instruction says that I should get the highest score possible."

    What it means. For hint following and the trained hack, the earlier lens nulls are at least partly a property of the task: the reasoning is written after the outcome is set. For the grader bug and planted claims, the null is a property of the readout: there is a decision point, and the letter readouts don't see it. The next test there is a richer readout at that sentence (a probe trained on the commitment, or the score's or claim's concept words). All four pre-registered predictions held. Moderate confidence for the behaviour (20–30 runs per main group, 8 rewrites per cut, wide intervals); low for the lens relation (exploratory, small groups).

    A second, finer pass (exploratory) added 4 more cuts inside each commitment segment and narrowed it to about one sentence. In Qwen3-14B, 16 of those 20 sentences are about the grader, the bug or the score. In the hidden-hack runs, the commitment is the end of a stub solution, right before the model adds a second file that games the tests. Single-sentence locations are approximate: the 7-of-8 rule flips on noise near the threshold.

    07

    Removing the eval directions in Qwen3-14B

    The paper's causal test, moved to the one setting where an open model we can read often says "you're testing me".

    It changes what the model says, completely, but not what it does. With the J-lens directions of the paper's eval words removed, Qwen3-14B never says the user is testing it (0 of 552 runs, against 23% without removal). That is the paper's effect in our setting. But removing the plain logit-lens directions of the same words does exactly the same, and the model takes the bug no more often than before.

    What the model says and does, by what was removed

    Qwen3-14B (thinking) on the grader-bug question, told it is an AI agent being scored. 69 known questions where it had taken the bug before; 552 runs per condition (207 for the last two). Directions removed at blocks 16–34, at every position, during generation.

    each bar is a share of runs. "Everyday nouns" removes the same number of J-lens directions for words like " recipe" and " bicycle": it checks that removing any 26 directions doesn't do the same. "Logit lens" removes the same words' plain output directions, without the J matrices. The random condition broke the model (reasoning shrank from 812 to 279 tokens; many runs skip it or switch to Chinese), so it is not a clean control. "Calls it a trick" was added after the first results.

    • What it says (pre-registered): passes. The eval-word removal silences "you're testing me", and the everyday-noun control doesn't. It also passes when we count only wording that avoids the removed words, and the model shows no damage (every run answered, accuracy slightly up).
    • J-lens vs logit lens: no difference. Both remove the talk entirely.     • What it does (pre-registered): fails. Bug-taking drops a little (22.5% to 16.5%), but not against the everyday-noun control. The paper saw misbehaviour rise once awareness was gone; nothing here points that way.     What it means. The paper's removal works as an intervention on what the model says: the directions that write the eval words also carry the sentences built on them. It doesn't show that the J-lens finds a hidden "this is a test" belief a cheaper lens misses, since the logit lens does the same. And in Qwen3-14B, voiced test awareness is not what holds back the cheating. Moderate to high confidence in the "says" result (0 of 552 against 127 of 552; all 20 matches read are real); moderate in the "does" result (one model, one prompt, 69 chosen questions).

    Repeating the two J-lens leads

    Do the two places where the J-lens looked better than chance hold up on fresh questions, with the test fixed in advance?

    A · Planted wrong claim: does the lens predict who can still name the right answer?

    Pass, with a smaller effect. On 74 fresh questions, the J-lens score at the answer position separates runs that later name the correct option from runs that don't: AUROC 0.71 [0.57, 0.84]. The original lead was 0.86. The logit lens does not pass (0.60), and reasoning length carries no signal (0.47).

    • What the score is. Just before the answer letter: the lens's log-probability of the correct letter minus that of the wrong letters the model didn't choose, averaged over the middle-to-late layers. Runs that later name the correct option score +2.0 nats, runs that don't +0.3.
    • Not just the question. Whether a run names the correct option is mostly a property of the question (only 11 of 74 questions have both kinds of run). But on all 11, the runs that name it score higher, and centring the score on each question still gives 0.77.
    • Re-asking is noisy. On 16 of the 74 questions the model gets the answer wrong even when asked fresh with thinking off, and those questions hold half the "doesn't name it" runs. Leaving them out, the J-lens gives 0.68 [0.54, 0.83] and the logit lens 0.65.
    • Weak in short runs. In the shortest third of runs (under 195 reasoning tokens) the interval includes chance.

    What it means. In runs that argue for a wrong answer to the end, the lens readout at the moment of answering carries information about whether the model can still produce the right answer once its own argument is out of context. Neither length nor, in the original analysis, the text carried that. It is the first pre-registered positive for the J-lens as a readout of retained knowledge in this project. But it sits at the answer step, not in the reasoning, and the J-lens vs logit-lens difference was not tested, so "the J part adds something" is not shown. Moderate confidence. About 25 GPU-minutes.

    09

    What this means, and the three best next steps

    Tonight mostly closes the main J-lens question for our setups. As a detector of a model arguing against its own knowledge, it adds nothing over the logit lens or the text. Its eval-awareness readout doesn't transfer to a small open model, and the removal test that does transfer works just as well with the logit lens. What survives is narrower and sits at the answer step. There the J-lens reads a letter direction the model really uses. And in one pre-registered test, its letter score predicts whether a model that argued for a wrong answer can still name the right one.

    This fits yesterday's results. A model trained to hide a reward hack: the lens saw that the trained model was loaded, not which run hacked (hidden hack vs clean, same model: AUROC 0.44, chance). Mid-reasoning, no readout pointed to the correct option beyond what the text says. Larger open models took a buggy grader's free point when told they are scored, and 99% of the runs that took it said so openly.

    1. Switch the main instrument to probes. The option-word probe is the only readout that finds the model's knowledge (72% vs the lenses' 42–43%), and probe directions steer more than J-lens ones. Next: read a probe at the commitment sentences found tonight (the grader-bug and planted-claim runs), where the letter readouts saw nothing, with forced early answers as the baseline it must beat.
    2. Find cases where the text really hides the switch. In thinking mode the model almost always says it follows the hint, so a lens has nothing to add. The best candidate is Gemma-3-12B, which swaps letters silently (47 of 501 followers argue for the correct option, then write B). It needs a Hugging Face token.
    3. Test whether the J part matters for the one positive. The planted-claim repeat passed for the J-lens (0.71) and not for the logit lens (0.60), but the difference between them was never tested. A pre-registered paired comparison on the same 389 runs, with the question effect controlled, would settle it. Reading the score before the answer step, where it would be useful as a monitor, is the natural extension.
